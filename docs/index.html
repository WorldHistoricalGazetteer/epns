DEEP: the English place-name survey, digitised

Every name in the county volumes of the English Place-Name Society survey that had been published by 2013, with the historical spellings and dated sources that the printed volumes give for each: 539,372 places and 429,536 dated citations of their spellings, from Domesday Book onwards, digitised in 2011–13 by DEEP (Digital Exposure of English Place-names) and published in 2017 by Jisc, the UK higher-education technology body.

What this is

The Digital Exposure of English Place-names project (DEEP, November 2011 to October 2013, funded by Jisc and run by the Institute for Name-Studies at Nottingham with the Centre for Data Digitisation and Analysis at Queen's University Belfast and the University of Edinburgh) converted the printed survey into structured XML, which Jisc released in 2017 as the 66 files this site reads. It contains, for each place, the survey's headword, its position in the administrative hierarchy (county, hundred or wapentake, parish, township), the variant spellings the editors collected, and for each spelling the dates and sources in which it is attested, transcribed from the volumes' own apparatus. For a minority of places it also carries coordinates supplied by up to four gazetteers.

This is a second route into that material, not a replacement for the first. The Institute for Name-Studies has continued to develop its Digital Survey of English Place-Names since 2013, now covering later volumes too, with a reading interface at Nottingham and, for etymologies and name elements, the Key to English Place-Names. What this site adds is the 2017 release as open, machine-readable data: every record as PLATO, Linked Places Format and the original MADS, whole-corpus downloads, persistent identifiers that carry DEEP's own numbering, and a map. The 2013 project's own record URLs no longer resolve (the placenames.org.uk domain has changed hands), which is why the identifiers here matter.

Searching

  • Type a modern name or a historical spelling. The index holds every headword, every variant spelling and the survey's own normalised forms (so Bunstow(e) is found by Bunstowe or Bunstow). Matching ignores case, accents and punctuation but keeps æ, þ and ð in the results as written.
  • Phonetic matching finds spellings that sound like what you typed even when no letters agree, which is how a medieval scribe's Snotingaham meets Nottingham. It uses Symphonym v8, a phonetic embedding model, run entirely in your browser: the first use downloads the model and a precomputed index (about 50 MB), after which both are cached. Results are ranked by sound (Symphonym cosine, 0.70 or better) and a candidate is withheld when it is not spelt anything like what you typed (bigram similarity under 0.35, with a lift when your word is contained in the name), the way the World Historical Gazetteer's own reconciliation tool vetoes a bad match; hover a score to see the spelling similarity too. One line per place, showing its best-matching form. It offers candidates; it does not decide.
  • Field-names are seven in ten of the records: the names of fields and closes listed under each township, with no spellings or coordinates of their own. Untick the box to keep them out of results.

The map, and what it does not show

Only places carry coordinates in the data, and those come from four different gazetteers (the survey's own, the Key to English Place-Names, Unlock and GeoNames) which often disagree by some distance. Each candidate is listed on the place's page; the dot on the map is the first available in that order of preference. Places without coordinates inherit their township's or parish's for the purpose of flying the map there, and are marked as such. Nothing here is a survey-grade location.

Basemaps: CARTO on OpenStreetMap, OpenStreetMap itself, or the Ordnance Survey six-inch second edition (1888–1913) served by the National Library of Scotland under CC BY 3.0, whose sheets show the landscape roughly as the earliest survey volumes were being compiled.

Reading an entry

Spellings are listed by date (one row per citation, ordered by the start of the editors' year-bracket, undated last, a recurring run at its first date) or as printed (the form in bold, then its citations, in the volume's order); the toggle remembers your choice. Hover, tap or tab to any attestation for a plain-English gloss of every token in it. The conventions, in brief:

  • Dates. A bare year is the date of the document. c. is circa; a. and p. are ante and post, before and after; 13th or 13 is the century; t. Hy 3 is tempore, in the reign of Henry III, and e., m., l. before a reign or century mean early, mid, late. The editors' year-bracket for each is in the tooltip.
  • Copy-dates. A date in brackets after the date, as in 921 (c. 925) ASC (A) or c. 1127 (l13), is when the surviving copy was made: the spelling is read in a late-13th-century copy, not the 1127 original. A letter in brackets after the source names the manuscript.
  • Sources. The abbreviation is the volume's; the tooltip expands the standard national ones (DB Domesday Book, FF Feet of Fines, Ass Assize Rolls, Pat Patent Rolls…) and says when it cannot. Italics mark an unpublished manuscript source, roman type a printed edition or calendar, the survey's own convention. ib. means the volume said ibidem and the encoders resolved it.
  • After the source: page, item, folio or entry number; (p), the spelling occurs within a personal name rather than as a reference to the place; (3 X), it occurs three times; et passim to, it recurs in sources between the two citations shown.

The identifier under each headword is the DEEP record id and is stable for citation. The 2013 project's own per-record URLs no longer resolve.

What is not here: etymologies

The DEEP XML carries names, hierarchy, spellings, sources and coordinates, but not the volumes' discussion of what each name means or which elements it contains. Those etymologies are in the Institute for Name-Studies' Key to English Place-Names, which does not expose per-place addresses to link to; each place page here offers a link to it for searching by name.

Storage

Data files are cached in your browser (IndexedDB) after the first download, so later visits are quick and the site works with a poor connection.

Downloads

The whole corpus is available as PLATO (lossless: 1,414,328 attestations in PLATO's sense, every sourced claim, of which the 429,536 dated citations of spellings are one kind), as Parquet tables and a DuckDB database (for analysis), and as Linked Places Format (lossy, with the losses stated) on the downloads page. Each place page also offers its own record in the three formats.
